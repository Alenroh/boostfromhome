# Across the Bridge

A literary novel in close third person about **Mastro Titta** — Giovanni Battista Bugatti, the official executioner of the Papal States from the 1790s through the 1860s. A real historical figure who performed more than five hundred executions across nearly seventy years, wore a red cloak on work days, lived in Trastevere, crossed the Tiber to Rome only when there was work, took deep Catholic pride in mercy through skill, and kept a journal of his life's labor.

The book is twelve months in his life. The interest is in the craft. The drama is the work itself.

---

## Time window

Twelve months in the mid-1820s under Pope Leo XII. Carbonari unrest gives steady work without forcing a single dramatic case. Bugatti is in his late 40s, a master of his profession, married to Maria, no children. By this point in his career he has been at it roughly thirty years.

---

## Structure

A calendar. Each section is a date. Dense weeks alternate with empty ones. The book opens in January — winter, no executions yet, the man in his life. The first dispatch arrives in February. The year begins.

The book ends in December with the year's final case (or its absence — see the reprieve).

---

## Voice and register

Close third, restrained. Bresson-quiet. McCarthy without the cosmic horror; Marilynne Robinson if she wrote about a killer. The book holds its register; the man holds his.

**Interior voice — small and grounded.** Bugatti is principled and methodical, but he is not a Bressonian abstraction. He is annoyed by mud. He likes good bread. He thinks about the price of grain. He prays without strain. *Digging in the mud sucks.* His thoughts are the size of a man's thoughts, not a novel's thoughts.

**No arc. No journey. No transformation.** The book begins; he is who he is. The book ends; he is who he is. Twelve months of a man with a job and a life. A sliver.

---

## The two channels

The book is two channels running in parallel that almost never collide.

### Channel A — the work

A dozen-plus executions across the year. Each gets a few pages of full procedural texture: the warrant arriving the night before, the prayer, the sharpening, the cloak with its tassels, the walk to the bridge, the crowd, the snuff offered to the condemned, the positioning of the body.

Then the act itself is **one sentence:**

> *The head was separated from the shoulders.*
>
> *The blade fell clean.*
>
> *The mannaia did its work.*
>
> *He was beheaded at the seventh hour.*
>
> *The garrote was applied.*
>
> *He was hanged at noon.*

Then the cleaning, the digging, and that evening's **journal entry** — short, clipped, quality-checking:

> *It wasn't as swift as usual. The man was thicker through the neck than expected. Note: for men of his build, double the angle.*
>
> *The coins on the eyes were Persian. A simple mistake. Will never happen again.*
>
> *The crowd was light. A rain morning. The condemned wept twice and recovered. He died well.*

### Channel B — the man

A small life. **Maria.** The farm at the edge of Trastevere — a few rooms, a half-acre with a vineyard and two goats. He works it on non-work days.

He is also an **umbrella maker** — paper parasols, painted by hand, the same hands that work the blade. He has a workbench. He takes commissions. Known in Trastevere for his work.

Friends he plays cards with on Tuesdays. Mass on Sunday. The tavern in the evening.

**Episodic, no plot.** A card game. A storm that nearly takes the vines. A good harvest he thanks God for at mass. A friend's wedding. An umbrella commission for a young bride. A Tuesday-night tavern argument about the price of bread. He helps a neighbor mend a fence. He buys a fish at the market. His mother is alive somewhere and well.

---

## The craftsmanship — pages devoted to the work

The book commits real space to the procedural rituals. Not as filler. As the subject. Bugatti's pride is in the labor, and the book renders that labor the way Patrick O'Brian rendered rope work, or Tolstoy a man scything wheat. **The reader is taught to see this work as work.**

Concrete passages that should get half a page or more each, scattered across the year:

- **Sharpening the axe** — whetstone, angle, the test (a piece of paper, a hair from his own head), wiping, oiling, wrapping in oilcloth. Saturday evenings in the cellar.
- **The mannaia** — blade, runners, rope, counterweight. He checks the drop the day before each use. He greases the runners with a small horn of tallow. He tests the trigger three times.
- **Tying the hangman's noose** — the thirteen turns of the knot, the placement under the left ear, the drop calculated by the condemned's weight (he keeps a small leather notebook of weights and corresponding rope lengths).
- **The trapdoor** — tested the day before. The hinges oiled monthly. The bolt replaced last year.
- **Carrying the stool from the shed** — a beat-up wooden stool with one shortened leg, used for thirty years.
- **The mazzola** — the great two-handed mallet. Heavy, demanding practice. He keeps a sand-filled cloth head-form in the cellar and practices monthly. Maria has stopped commenting.
- **The garrote** — kept oiled in a cloth in the cedar chest. Rarely used.
- **The cloak** — papal red, tasseled, folded with care after each use. Maria mends it. He brushes it Saturday mornings.
- **The bag of tools** — packed in the same order each time. Sharpening stones, oilcloth, rope, blade-cover, prayer beads, snuffbox for the condemned.
- **Cleaning the blade** — after. With oil and cloth. Sometimes an hour. He does not let anyone else touch it.
- **Digging the grave** — depth, narrowness, the prayer he says when shoveling the first earth back over the body.

These passages are not arrayed in one chapter. They are scattered across the year — one or two per execution, sometimes a quiet domestic scene where the implements are tended without a coming use. The reader gradually understands that **this is what he does.** The executions are punctuation. The work is everything.

A doctor scrubbing his hands. The motion is ritual. The motion is technique. The motion is care. Bugatti is the doctor.

---

## Spectacle variation — by status of the condemned

The book teaches the reader to recognize the case type from the dispatch's tone and the crowd's behavior. By the seventh or eighth execution, the dispatch's first lines tell the reader what kind of day this will be.

**1. Quotidian.** Common criminal, brief dispatch, small crowd, fast ritual. Charges read by a junior clerk. A short prayer. Last rites administered by a single Confraternity friar. Then the work. Bugatti's full craft is identical to higher-profile cases — the difference is who is watching. These account for perhaps half the year's docket. Sometimes multiple condemned at once.

**2. Standard public.** Bandits, common murderers, the upper tier of ordinary crime. Larger crowd. Full Confraternity procession from the chapel of San Giovanni Decollato to the scaffold. Charges read aloud by a senior clerk. Papal prayer (a designated friar). Last rites. Snuff offered. Execution.

**3. Political.** The dispatch is the heaviest — theological justifications, sedition against the Vicar of Christ, the Holy See's duty. Short papal prayer (sometimes in absentia, as a written blessing). Heads on spikes at the city gates after, displayed for three days. Foreign observers present — English, French, Austrian.

**4. High-profile carnival.** Nobles, prominent figures, famous bandits. Full carnival. Extra cardinals attend. Foreign ambassadors. Raised stands for the elite. Carnival vendors. English tourists with their carriages and notebooks. The execution is reported in London and Paris papers. Bugatti wears his best; Maria mends the cloak beforehand.

**5. Sacred / sacrilege.** A defrocked friar, a blasphemer, a thief of consecrated objects. Particular ecclesiastical processing. Sometimes unusual implements (garrote for clergy). Quieter, uncomfortable crowds. Heavy ecclesiastical weight.

**6. Mass execution.** A group condemned together. The procession is collective. Each man gets his ritual but it's compressed. The blade is sharpened mid-event. The crowd thins over the morning.

---

## The papal dispatches

Each execution is preceded by the official church warrant — formal, institutional, third-person Latin-flavored prose describing the crime and the condemned. The reader learns each man's story through the church's words first. Then Bugatti's procedural pages. Then the one sentence. Then his quality-check entry. **Four registers braided together.**

The dispatches vary by crime category:

- **Banditry** — emphasizes safety of travelers and pilgrims, order
- **Parricide** — invokes natural law, the patriarchal bond, the violation of God's order
- **Carbonari / political** — heaviest theological justification, sedition against the Vicar of Christ
- **Uxoricide** — invokes the sacrament of marriage, the husband's violation
- **Infanticide** — language of innocent life, the violation of motherhood
- **Sacrilege** — full ecclesiastical weight, particular outrage
- **Theft** — spare, procedural, almost actuarial

---

## Method variety

Not only the mannaia (Roman guillotine). The book renders each method with the same procedural care.

- **Mannaia** — the standard, for both nobles and commoners
- **Hanging** — for lesser crimes, on a low platform
- **Mazzolatura** — the great two-handed mallet to the head, followed by throat-cutting, for the worst marital crimes
- **Squartamento** — quartering, after mazzolatura, for the worst compounded crimes; body parts displayed at the city gates
- **Garrote** — for clergy, in keeping with old custom; rare
- **Beheading by axe** — older, less common by this era

---

## Channels brushing softly

Not dramatic intersection. Moments like *Carlo from the tavern was in the crowd today. He did not stay for the end.* The book registers and moves on. No tavern friend appears on the docket. No dramatic recognition. The book holds its strange equilibrium: a man who does *this* and also *this.*

## The execution roster — full pool of 20 options

Curate to a final 12–15. The roster is ordered chronologically across the year.

### 1. February — Antonio Calvi, 31. Highway robbery.

Captured outside Tivoli after killing a papal courier. Common case, common crime. The dispatch is brief and procedural — safety of travelers and pilgrims. Hanging in the Piazza del Popolo. A small Tuesday crowd. Journal that evening notes the morning was cold and the rope ran clean.

**The book's first execution. The reader learns the form here.**

### 2. Late February — Tramonti brothers and a third accomplice. Banditry. Group hanging.

Captured together in the hills near Anagni. The dispatch is brief — banditry, three names. Hanging in succession. Bugatti executes one, cleans the rope, executes the next, cleans again, executes the third. The crowd thins.

**The quotidian register: the working day.** Journal: *Three. The youngest was thinner than the rope. Note: bring the lighter trapdoor next time for slight builds.*

### 3. March — Pietro Furi, 24. Parricide.

Killed his father with an axe after a long argument over a vineyard. Drunken, regretful, willing to die. The dispatch is heavier — natural law, the patriarchal bond, the violation of God's order. Mannaia in the Piazza Navona. The young man weeps at the scaffold.

Journal: *He was repentant. The blade fell clean. Note: the platform was wet from morning rain.*

### 4. Late March — Count Lodovico Carragosti, 44. Carbonari, noble.

The high-profile carnival of the spring. A landed count from the Romagna implicated in financing the network. The dispatch is the longest yet — full theological framing, full aristocratic protocol. Cardinals in attendance, foreign ambassadors, raised stands. Mannaia. The count is calm and contemptuous to the last.

Journal: *His silk was very fine. His neck was no different.*

### 5. April — Giuseppe Verrina, 38. Carbonari conspiracy.

Lawyer, intellectual, articulate, refuses last rites. The first political case of the year. The dispatch is the longest of the spring — theological justifications, sedition against the Vicar of Christ, the Holy See's duty to suppress secret societies. Mannaia in the Piazza del Popolo. Heavy crowd. The English tourists are present.

Journal that evening is uncharacteristically brief. *Politics. The blade fell clean.*

### 6. May — Niccolò Maranza, 42. Uxoricide.

Laborer, killed his wife with a hammer in a drunken fight. Mazzolatura — the great mallet to the head — followed by throat-cutting, the public penalty for the violation of the marriage sacrament. Heavy work: two implements, two motions.

Journal: *Two strokes were needed. The first was not as clean as I would have preferred. Note: more practice with the mazzola.*

### 7. May — Adele Castagno, 34. Poisoning.

Killed her husband and his brother with arsenic over six months. Calculated, intelligent, refused to plead. The dispatch carries particular language about female deceit and the violation of household trust. Hanging. The crowd is uncomfortable.

Journal: *She walked to the platform without help. Her hand did not shake. I have seen this once before.*

### 8. June — Mariangela Costa, 19. Infanticide.

A servant in a noble household, killed her newborn whose father may have been her employer. The case is delicate. The dispatch is unusually short — the violation of motherhood, the innocent life. Hanging in the early morning. The Confraternity processes her with extra care. The crowd is small and unusually quiet.

Journal: *She wept all the way to the platform. The drop was true. The work was sorrowful. Maria asked about it. I did not answer.*

### 9. Early July — Domenico Ferri, 29. Counterfeiter.

Economic crime. The dispatch is dry, factual, almost actuarial — the harm to commerce, the harm to the papal mint. Hanging on a low platform. The crowd is sparse.

**The quotidian register — the kind of execution the city barely notices.** Journal: *He was very thin. The rope ran long. Nothing remarkable.*

### 10. July — Vincenzo and Tomasso Falcone, 27 and 24. Banditry and sacrilege.

Brothers convicted of a coordinated highway operation that included the killing of a priest. The compounded sin is the priest's death. Mannaia, twin executions back-to-back, the older brother first. The dispatch is doubled — two near-identical formal documents. The crowd is massive.

Journal: *A long morning. Two clean strokes. The mannaia held. Maria laid out my best cloak that evening.*

### 11. August — Sergeant Mario Bertoli, 28. Papal guardsman, deserter and killer.

Stabbed the corporal sent to retrieve him from his hometown. Military execution by garrote at the Castel Sant'Angelo. Smaller, semi-private ceremony. The dispatch is military in tone rather than ecclesiastical.

**A different register entirely — the army's grief, the city's indifference.** Journal: *He was a soldier. He died like one. Note: the garrote needed cleaning again. I will do it Sunday.*

### 12. August — Brother Domenico Fabiani, 51 (defrocked Capuchin). Sacrilege.

Stole consecrated hosts from his own monastery and sold them on the open market. Defrocked in a ceremony the week before execution. The dispatch is the heaviest of the year — full ecclesiastical weight, the violation of the Most Blessed Sacrament. Garrote, in keeping with old custom for clergy. The crowd is uncomfortable.

Journal: *He was a man of God once. The garrote took longer than the mannaia would have. Custom is custom.*

### 13. September — Carlo Vendetti, 22. Carbonari.

Captured in Rome, refused to inform. Young, dignified, articulate, took communion before the walk. The dispatch repeats much of the Verrina language. Mannaia in the Piazza del Popolo.

Journal: *Another young one. The blade held its edge through both this morning and the courier last week. Note: I will sharpen it Saturday.*

### 14. Late September — Francesco Beccari. Bandit, recaptured.

A man Bugatti executed by hanging in 1817 — the rope broke, the man was pardoned per custom, sent home, went back to banditry, caught again eight years later. The reading of charges names the prior pardon. Hanging again, this time with a heavier rope. The crowd murmurs.

Journal: *The same man. I did not expect to see him again. This time the drop was true. Note: rope thickness matters more than I'd thought at his weight.*

### 15. October — Pasquale Trincia, 39. Banditry and multiple murder.

The carnival. Bandit chief responsible for at least nine deaths across the Lazio countryside. The dispatch lists his crimes by victim. The full ceremonial: mazzolatura, throat-cutting, quartering, body parts displayed at the gates. Enormous crowd. English tourists with their carriages and notebooks. Long, ugly work.

**Carlo from the tavern is in the crowd.** Bugatti writes only that line about Carlo in his journal, between two longer technical paragraphs about the quartering. **The reader sees the channels brush.**

### 16. November — Aldo Buonatesta, 63. Old-case banditry.

A chief whose career ended fifteen years ago when he vanished into rural life. Found out by a tip from a former rival. Tried and convicted swiftly. Mannaia. The man is old, calm, indifferent — he has prepared for this morning for a decade and a half. He asks for nothing.

Journal: *He was ready. The work was easy. I prefer them ready.*

### 17. November — Carmelo and Domitilla Speziale. Husband-and-wife bandits.

Executed together. Mannaia for both. The crowd is enormous — unusual to see a woman of any station beheaded. The Confraternity processes them as a couple, one friar on each. Bugatti executes the husband first at the wife's request.

**A second carnival of the year, harder than the count's.** Journal: *She was steady. She watched him die and did not weep. I sharpened the blade between them. Maria did not ask why I came home late.*

### 18. December — Vincenzo Macchi, 19. Theft, manslaughter, sacrilege.

A village boy whose family is starving. Stole from a church poor-box and killed the sacristan in the ensuing fight. Three charges compounded. The youngest of the year. The dispatch is the briefest — fact, fact, fact. Hanging in early morning. Small cold crowd.

Journal: *He was a child. The work was the work. Note: his shoes were poor. I gave Maria the coin to send food to his village.*

### 19. Late December — Five Carbonari. Mass political execution.

Late-year arrests in Bologna yielded a major cell. Five men, twenties and thirties, brought to Rome for execution together. Mannaia, five in succession across a brutal morning. The papal court in attendance. Foreign ambassadors. The crowd massive and divided — Carbonari sympathizers murmuring, papal loyalists cheering. The papal dispatch is the longest of the year, naming all five with biographical detail.

**The worst day of his year.** Journal: *Five. The blade was sharpened twice. Maria did not ask. I did not speak.*

### 20. Late December — Stefano Olivetti, 28. Forgery and attempted assassination. THE REPRIEVE.

Dispatch issued, procession formed, scaffold prepared. The papal pardon arrives at dawn. Bugatti has walked across the bridge with his cloak. He stands at the scaffold while the announcement is read. Olivetti weeps. Bugatti packs his tools and walks back.

**The reader experiences the full procedural setup and the absence of the act.** Journal: *The pardon came. The man wept. I have not seen that often.*

## A possible final 15

- **Core** (1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 13, 15, 18, 19, 20) — gives variety of method, register, and time of year
- **Plus 2 of:** the group hanging (2), the poisoner (7), the counterfeiter (9), the deserter (11), the recaptured man (14), the old bandit (16), the husband-wife pair (17)

Or curate differently. Method coverage of any final list should include hanging, mannaia, mazzolatura, garrote, plus the reprieve. Status coverage should include the full range — quotidian, standard, political, high-profile carnival, sacred, mass.

---

## What the book is not

- No character arc, no journey, no transformation
- No crisis of conscience, no third-act conversion
- No dramatic execution of a friend, no death of his mother, no narrative incident
- The interest is in the *steadiness* — the way a man of faith and craft does necessary terrible work day after day for forty years and remains, somehow, recognizably himself

---

## Satirical edges

The English tourists who travel to watch. The carnival vendors at executions. The cardinals who sign warrants and avert their eyes from him at mass.

---

## Title

*Across the Bridge.* The bridge is the only place the two channels brush against each other — he crosses it, in red, on work mornings. The rest of the time he is on one side or the other. The bridge is not a metaphor. He just walks it. The book is the year of his walking it.
