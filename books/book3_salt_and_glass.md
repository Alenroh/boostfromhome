# Salt and Glass

A picaresque slice-of-life — six to eight months in the working life of **Captain Renn**, a *finder* in an island archipelago where magical artifacts are part of daily life. Episodic, no overarching quest. He has a job; he does it. The artifacts are the texture of the world, not the prize.

Era: 1780s–1820s. Age of sail. Candles and oil lamps, letters by post, no telegraph, no steam. Patrick O'Brian / Hornblower era.

---

## The world — the archipelago

### Shape and feel

A scattered cluster of islands across a temperate sea. **New Zealand-flavored.** Cool maritime climate. Heavy rain. Gales. Long winters with brief brilliant summers. Mountains running down to the sea on the wild coasts; gentler farmland on the sheltered ones. Geothermal areas on the volcanic islands. The outer islands are cold, sub-antarctic, fewer than a thousand people on each.

Less marble, more slate. Less white-and-blue, more gray-and-green. Less wine, more whiskey.

Economy: sheep, timber, whaling and fishing, geothermal industries, a little wine in the warmest spots. Stone houses with slate roofs, working ports, wool stores, salting houses.

Culture: frontier-stoic. Plainspoken. **The Kiwi farmer-fisherman sensibility, scaled up to a whole society.**

### The fallen empire — the Northern Crown

The **Northern Crown** — a maritime kingdom from across the eastern sea that colonized the archipelago two centuries ago. Ruled through governors and a colonial bureaucracy. Its power was built on three things: a powerful navy, a competent administration, and a network of artifacts that allowed it to govern at scale — truth-compasses for magistrates, scrying mirrors for the postal service, ledger-stones that audited themselves, locator-instruments for shipping.

**The empire didn't fall in a war. It fell in an audit.**

In the empire's last decades, the artifacts began producing too many wrong answers. Slowly, with each generation. The compasses lied a little more. The mirrors showed slightly older versions of events. The ledger-stones drifted toward small errors that the bureaucracy compounded. By the time the Crown's tax accountants finally realized that the imperial books had been wrong for fifteen years, the garrisons hadn't been paid in two, and the army had begun to dissolve quietly back to its home villages.

A war with the kingdom across the eastern sea, going badly, finished what the audit started. Within five years the empire was a name with no power behind it.

The collapse, roughly twenty-five years before the book begins, left the islands intact but un-governed. **The empire died of its own slightly-broken machinery. The book's central satire lives in this history.** Over-engineered solutions that almost worked. *Almost* is what killed the empire. The Crown still exists at home, somewhere, faded and irrelevant; the colonies have moved on.

### The League of the Free Islands

In the empire's place: the **League of the Free Islands.** A loose federation that meets four times a year, rotating its conference among the larger members. It handles only what it must — anti-piracy patrols, trade disputes, weights and measures, the lighthouses nobody else will fund. Each island governs itself; the League is a forum, not a state.

Some imperial offices still exist in zombie form — a postal service, a customs union, a lighthouse-keepers' guild. Underfunded, decrepit, beloved.

### Religion and the Order

The mainline religion is **Reformed and austere.** Plain churches with gray stone walls and slate roofs and no images. Sermons matter more than sacraments.

**The Order — the Order of the Pelican — is separate from the mainline Church.** A working monastic-craftsman tradition, older than the empire itself, possibly indigenous to the islands. Quiet, skilled, slightly suspect, generally tolerated. The Order has its mother house on Stonereach.

The artifacts were originally made by the Order. They still make some — sometimes the most reliable available, sometimes worse than what their predecessors made three centuries ago. They do not advertise which is which.

Secular guild artificers across the archipelago make cheap imitations, badly. Those are the dangerous ones.

### The twelve islands

- **Crownport** — former imperial capital. Faded grandeur, marble palaces partitioned into apartments. Naval academy still functions. Bureaucratic, polite, slightly mournful. Renn served on Crownport as a young officer.
- **Stonereach** — the Order's mother house. Olive groves, white walls, quiet. The Magister, **Master Halloran**, is a sharp-witted man in his seventies.
- **Greyport** — the trading entrepôt. Cosmopolitan, mercantile. Renn's cousin **Vienne** works at the customs house here.
- **Black Sound** — the lawless island, fjord-cut, a hideout for smugglers. The tavern is called **The Bone Bowl.**
- **Hookhaven** — the fishing-village island. Hard-working, conservative.
- **Marrowmoor** — sheep country, the wool-and-mutton aristocracy. **Mrs. Halverson** runs the largest estate.
- **Westwold** — the academic island. The university and three guild halls of bad-artifact artificers. Renn's rival finder, **Marcus Halvey**, lives here.
- **Iron Bay** — the naval island. The League's modest fleet. **Admiral Liana Voss** is a friend from Renn's navy years.
- **The Old Holdfast** — decommissioned imperial penal colony, used as a place to house people no one wants.
- **Coldhope** — the far outer rocks. Sub-antarctic, fewer than a thousand people.
- **Furnace Island** — volcanic, semi-active, sulfur mines and hot springs. Produces dark glass used in many artifacts.
- **Marsola** — Renn's home. Small, working, unglamorous. About three thousand people. The tavern is **The Lapwing.**

### League politics

Background tensions, surface only when relevant:
- Crownport would like to be the capital again. The other islands resist.
- Black Sound is technically a member of the League but pays no dues. Periodic debates about expulsion go nowhere.
- Westwold's guild artificers want League regulation of artifact-making to restrict the Order's competition. The Magister has politely refused.
- Mrs. Halverson holds the swing vote on most League matters because she controls the largest private fleet.

## Captain Renn

### Visual

**Geoffrey Rush as Barbossa in *Pirates of the Caribbean,* minus the peg leg.** Weathered face, sharp eyes, dark hair gone gray at the temples, beard. Not handsome in a leading-man way. Charismatic in a worn way. The face of a man who has been through bad weather and is still here.

### Background

Born on a smaller island. Naval academy on Crownport. Junior officer during the late imperial years. Privateer with letters of marque during the empire's collapse. Finder ever since.

Has been to every island multiple times. Has scars from Black Sound, an account at a Greyport bank, a standing invitation to dinner with Mrs. Halverson (which he politely never accepts), and a corner table reserved for him at The Lapwing.

Was married once. The book doesn't dwell.

### Personality — between the Mandalorian and Tommy Shelby

The Mandalorian gives you: quiet, capable, honorable, lone-wolf, tight-lipped, professional, occasionally tender, works for hire, travels alone.

Tommy Shelby gives you: harder-edged, politically intelligent, willing to use violence cleverly, charismatic with women, drinks and smokes, plans steps ahead, working-class but powerful, war-wounded but doesn't dwell, cold when needed.

Renn is between them. He has more humor than Shelby and more warmth at the edges. He has more danger than Mando. He's a finder, not a kingpin, but the man you don't fuck with. **A healthy Rick — the wit, the danger, the competence, without the nihilism.**

### Character — how he handles situations

- **Trust violations are transactional.** A client lies, he either walks (trust broken, end of deal) or charges double for the inconvenience. He doesn't moralize about being lied to.
- **He uses leverage for vulnerable people without becoming a vigilante.** Within the job, he has discretion. He'll broker a settlement, extract a guarantee, slow-walk a delivery. He doesn't break the contract to become Robin Hood. He is not the Punisher.
- **Working honor.** A young man pulls a knife in a tavern; Renn could kill him; he doesn't. He tunes him up a little, or talks him down with the easy confidence of someone who could end it. He doesn't escalate. He doesn't pick on the weak. He doesn't keep score over small things.
- **Mistakes are owned, not carried.** A case goes wrong, someone dies because of a mistake he made: flowers on the grave, a present to the family, on with the work. **Principle, not penance.** He won't repeat the mistake. He won't dwell on it.
- **Care without drama.** Old Tessler is dying: books, food, drink, a paid nurse. Renn visits but maintains his own life. This is how working men care for each other.
- **He stops for kids.** A vulnerable child crosses his path; he engages, takes them somewhere safe, moves on. He doesn't adopt. He doesn't dramatize. He just doesn't walk past.
- **Romantically honest.** A casual lover wants more; he tells her plainly where he stands, or ends it cleanly. No drifting. No leading on.
- **Won't be a tool for injustice.** A magistrate orders him to bring in an innocent man for political reasons; Renn refuses and finds another job. **He has work he won't do.**
- **Friends in trouble: he shows up if he can.** No production. *Friend asks for help, you help him.* That's the loyalty mechanism.
- **The fisherman model.** One case at a time. Catches, dresses, cooks, processes, moves on. **Not Messina's concurrency.** Renn is present in what's in front of him. Between cases he's on Marsola — drinks, the boat, a woman if there is one, Tessler, the next case when it comes.
- **Pell stays minimal.** Renn teaches the kid knots and weather. He doesn't engage emotionally. The kid does not arc.
- **Formal dinners: reserved competence.** Goes when there's reason to. Behaves well. Listens more than he talks. Picks up what's useful. Leaves at a reasonable hour. **A rave is far too much.**
- **Wine with dinner.** That's the drinking. Regular, moderate, integrated. He drinks because he likes it, not to escape anything. Reliably steady.

### Lives in the present

He is not haunted. He is not reminiscent. The privateer years are not tendons under every page. He can mention something — *That reminds me of Carrick the year we took the Vrasna* — and then drop it. He does not pour out the memory. The reader catches a fragment and moves on.

### Religion is casual

He goes to mass when convenient. He talks to clergy the way Tommy Shelby talks to Father Hughes — professional, slightly fond, not reverent. The church is a social institution. He has not been to confession in years.

## The Petrel

Renn's cutter. Thirty-five feet, gaff-rigged, single mast with a topmast for setting a topsail in light air. Built ten years ago by a yard on Iron Bay. Kept at Marsola harbor.

He can sail her alone in decent weather. For rougher crossings or longer hauls, he hires **Pell**, a fifteen-year-old who lives with his grandmother and is saving money for his own boat.

For long voyages, he books passage on merchant ships — packet services, whaling tenders, the occasional naval cutter heading the right direction. The book renders different ships — different captains, different rules — without giving Renn a crew of his own. He is always a passenger, never a master.

## The contact network — the Mandalorian model

**Renn has a network, not a team.** A roster of contacts across the islands, each in their own corner, each with their own life. He comes to them when he needs them. He doesn't travel with them. None depend on him; he depends on most of them. **No Robin.** No sidekick. No traveling companion.

Twenty-two contacts:

1. **Vienne** — his young cousin, customs house, Greyport. Twenty-three, clever, refused her mother's marriage plans.
2. **Master Halloran** — Magister of the Order of the Pelican, Stonereach. Mid-seventies, sharp-witted, dry.
3. **Mrs. Halverson** — sheep-country widow, Marrowmoor. A hundred thousand head, a private fleet of wool-clippers. Shrewd and tired.
4. **Marcus Halvey** — rival finder, Westwold. They respect each other. Occasionally collaborate.
5. **Admiral Liana Voss** — League fleet, Iron Bay. Friend from the navy.
6. **Yarrow** — harbormaster at Marsola. Knows every boat, complains about the weather.
7. **Sister Margery** — lay sister on Hookhaven. Dry-witted, makes a particular spiced wine.
8. **Doss** — fence and smuggler on Black Sound. Owes Renn his life from a privateer-era incident neither of them mentions.
9. **Magistrate Wren** — Crownport. Hires Renn for politically delicate cases. Forty-something, fastidious, never says thank you.
10. **Mariko** — coffee-house proprietor, Greyport. Refugee from somewhere east. Knows everyone's gossip.
11. **Captain Vance** — retired whaling captain, Hookhaven. Reads weather better than anyone.
12. **Pell** — the fifteen-year-old who crews the long crossings.
13. **Old Tessler** — retired finder, Marsola. Renn's predecessor. Mid-seventies, drinks too much, dying. Will die before the book ends, off-page, mentioned in a single line.
14. **Bey Aro** — banker on Crownport. Holds Renn's modest savings.
15. **Mira Salvo** — calligrapher and forger, Westwold. Three cats and one very old dog.
16. **Ellam Carrick** — former privateer crewmate, Coldhope. Hard man, missing two fingers.
17. **The Widow Pratt** — boarding house, Greyport. Brisk, motherly, excellent stew.
18. **Tip** — twelve-year-old street kid, Greyport. Sharp, smart, future trouble.
19. **Dr. Hannit** — physician, Marrowmoor. Patches Renn up. Never asks questions.
20. **Eyrith Tan** — antiquarian, Westwold. Lives with another man in a house full of books. Nobody's business.
21. **Em Larik** — lighthouse keeper, Coldhope. Lives mostly alone with her dog. Eyes like a hawk.
22. **Mr. Bolt** — printer, Crownport. Knows what's about to be public news.

Plus **Father Idris** — clergy, somewhere — a Shelby-style drinking partner more than a confessor.

## The artifact catalog

**Most artifacts work fine** — that's why people use them. The book pays attention to the *quirky* ones because that's where the satire lives. Renn also carries plain tools: his light-footed boots, a regular lantern that just works, a compass that just points north.

The inverted catalog — twenty-four:

1. Binoculars that show everything except what you're looking for
2. A compass that points to the nearest person lying
3. A spyglass that shows the sea ten minutes ago
4. A lantern that only illuminates what nobody is looking at
5. A bottle that fills with whatever drink you don't want
6. A map that updates only of places you've already been
7. A rope that ties any knot perfectly except the one you ask for
8. A coin that lands on the side you didn't pick
9. A pen that writes only what you didn't intend to say
10. A door that opens to the place you most recently left
11. A key that fits every lock except the one in front of you
12. A whistle that summons whoever you weren't thinking of
13. A mirror that shows you as others see you
14. A ledger that records all transactions correctly except your own
15. A blade that cuts everything except what you want to cut
16. A pocket watch that runs perfectly — counting down the wearer's lifespan
17. A scale that weighs guilt rather than mass
18. An anchor that always holds — except in storms
19. A weathervane that points to where the storm is going after it has passed
20. A walking stick that finds the longest path home
21. A set of dice that roll a number meaningful to the roller — never the wanted number
22. A telescope that shows the stars as they will be in a hundred years
23. A signet ring that proves identity — but only of the previous owner
24. A music box that plays the song you'll never want to hear again

Renn personally owns six or seven, including the lying compass, the ten-minutes-ago spyglass, and the wrong-knot rope. The rest he encounters in client homes and notes for later.

## The satire

The book is full of *people misusing artifacts because they assume they work normally.*

- A merchant uses the lying compass to confirm his clerk is stealing. The compass points to his daughter, who lies routinely about small things. He fires the clerk anyway because he has already decided.
- A young woman uses the pen-that-writes-what-you-didn't-intend for love letters, thinking it will produce passion, and reveals her hesitations instead.
- A ship captain uses the ten-minutes-ago spyglass to spot pirates and sails right into them.

**Over-engineered solutions that almost work.** The world is full of small misfortunes from inverted tools wielded by people who didn't read the directions. Renn fixes some of these. Most he just watches.

## Plot engine

Six to eight cases across a season — spring storms to autumn. Each case largely self-contained. Some pay well. Some are favors. One or two he wishes he hadn't taken.

**The fisherman model:** one case at a time. He catches the fish, dresses it, cooks it, processes it, then takes the next one. He doesn't carry concurrent cases. Between cases there is real downtime — Marsola, the boat, friends, the next call when it comes.

His kit of artifacts grows across the book — he picks up the lying compass in case one and uses it in case seven; he acquires the wrong-knot rope in case four and it saves his life in case ten. **Magical rules-lawyering** is the pleasure — clever exploitations of broken tools by a man who understands them better than their owners do.

A typical case structure:

1. Renn is hired (often through Magistrate Wren or Vienne)
2. He sails *The Petrel* to the relevant island, or books passage for a longer crossing
3. He visits one or two contacts en route or at destination
4. He resolves the case (or doesn't)
5. He sails home

### Action

Working violence shows up in this book — drama-light like the others, but the action is here. A flintlock drawn in a confined room. A stalking through a market. A positioning on a rooftop. A fight on a wharf that lasts thirty seconds and ends with one man down and Renn walking away. **Each handled with the same restraint as the rest of the book.** No purple action prose. The action is competent, not exciting.

### Relaxation

Renn has a life. He drinks (wine with dinner, beer at the tavern). He smokes a pipe sometimes. He sleeps with women — sometimes, casually, honestly. He attends a working-man's celebration when invited. **An afternoon with a woman appears in the book, handled without melodrama and without prudery.** This is the book where adult life shows up, where the other two would be wildly against type to include it. His ceiling is a tavern wake or a working-man's wedding, not a rave or a Marrowmoor ball.

## Voice and prose

**POV: third-person close.** The prose stays in his head but lets the world also be observed independently.

### Qualities

- **Laconic.** Short, precise statements. Few qualifiers. Long sentences when the work requires them, but interior thought stays clipped.
- **Dry humor.** He doesn't make jokes. He notices what is funny because it IS, and the noticing is the joke.
- **Procedural but light.** Not Bugatti-precision. He plans the way an experienced operator plans — quickly, completely, without dwelling. A paragraph on loading a pistol, not three pages on its provenance.
- **Observational, never moralizing.** He sees corruption, weakness, vanity, and registers it without comment. He thinks about a man's worn boot or a woman's bitten thumbnail and the reader makes the conclusion he won't make on the page.
- **Pessimistic but kind.** He doesn't expect much from people. He treats them decently anyway.
- **Maritime mind.** He thinks in weather, in tides, in compass bearings. A bad conversation feels like a wind backing. A man under pressure feels like a topmast about to crack.
- **Past-present, not past-haunted.** Memories arrive as flickers, get noted, get put away.
- **Private.** Doors in his mind he keeps closed. The book respects them.

### What the prose does not do

- No big emotions on the page. Grief, fear, regret registered through behavior and what is not said.
- No moralizing. Renn does not judge his clients out loud. The prose does not judge them either.
- No long backstory dumps. His past is given in flickers across the book.
- No purple description. Weather, ships, people — concretely, plainly, without ornament.
- No epigrams. He does not speak in maxims.
- No nostalgia. He does not pine for the imperial years.

### Recurring textures

- **"He did not."** Often the most telling sentence on a page is what Renn did not do. *He did not stop. He did not ask. He did not say.* A quiet refrain.
- **One-word paragraphs.** His interior often resolves to a single word — *Interesting. Hm. Right. Later.* — sitting alone on a line.
- **The sea as frame.** Comparisons reach naturally for sea, sails, weather.
- **Specific tools.** When he handles an artifact, he handles it concretely.
- **Names of birds.** Renn knows seabirds. They appear in his observations.

## The register

The book's tone is **a Peaky Blinders episode set on a sailboat in a Patrick O'Brian world.** Working-class, hard, capable, occasionally tender, occasionally violent, plainspoken, dry. A man with a life. A man with a job.

The melancholy stays as a low note — in the silence around his marriage, in what he doesn't say about the privateer years, in the lighthouse keeper he visits once and doesn't speak of. But it's not the dominant key. **The dominant key is competent forward motion through a working life. He's not mourning. He's living.**

---

## Title

*Salt and Glass.* The salt is the sea and the trade; the glass is the artifacts. The two materials of Renn's life.
